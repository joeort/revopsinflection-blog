Last month a services lead looked at a strong Q4 pipeline and said the first thing they needed was the hard dates. The sales leader had just run down the deals expected to sign, said plainly that it would mean a lot of services work, and asked the room to remember Q4 last year, when several large wins landed at once. When another executive asked whether services had the capacity to handle it, the services lead went straight to dates.

The reason was specific. Last year's pain came from nearly every one of those customers having a license expiring in the same couple of months, so they all had to be live at the same time. If fewer of this year's deals are up against expiry dates like that, the services lead said, it gets much easier to thread through.

## **The quarter that books well and lands badly**

I call this the **Go-Live Pileup** : a cluster of committed deals whose customers all need to be live in the same window. A bookings forecast has no way to show it, because the forecast sorts deals by close date and the pileup is set by dates the customer controls.

Two other details from that call sharpen the point. One deal had services approved to start before the contract was signed, so start came before close. A past large project had already pushed other customers' schedules, which is why the ops lead asked for a scenario-based capacity plan and a customer comms plan, built from the deal dates once they were known.

I've lived the worse version of this. In a past hypergrowth role, we poured gas on new-logo acquisition so aggressively that sales outpaced onboarding capacity, implementations went badly, and churn spiked months later (more in "The Other Ditch" section of [Your Expansion Number Is Borrowed From Old New Logos](https://revopsinflection.substack.com/p/your-expansion-number-is-borrowed)). That post also called out the ratios planning teams leave out, like Support:Customer/ARR, and services capacity belongs on the same list.

## **Close, Start, Must-Be-Live**

Every committed deal carries three dates that matter to the business after the signature. The forecast call is built on the first one, and services needs all three.

### **Close**

Close is when bookings land. Sales owns it, finance and the board care about it most, and services often plans from it by default because it's the only date sitting on the opportunity. It breaks because close is a selling milestone, and nothing about a close date tells services when work begins or when it has to be finished.

**How to tell if you 're in it:** ask your services lead what date they plan from. If the answer is close date, your implementation schedule is running on a sales milestone.

### **Start**

Start is when implementation work begins, and services owns it with the customer. It can come before close, as with the early-start deal on that call, or well after it when the customer's data or team isn't ready.

Close and start drift apart in both directions, for the same reason a six-month sales cycle doesn't mean every deal closes in six months ([Cohort progression](https://revopsinflection.substack.com/p/cohort-progression-why-a-6-month)). It breaks when services treats close plus a standard lag as the start date and staffs to that.

**How to tell if you 're in it:** for last year's projects, chart start date minus close date. A wide spread means close date was never a good proxy for start.

### **Must-Be-Live**

Must-be-live is the date the customer has to be running, and the customer sets it. For a displacement it's the incumbent's license or contract end, and elsewhere it's a contractual or regulatory deadline or a season or event the customer can't move. This is the date the services lead was asking for, and those expiry dates decided whether last Q4 was painful.

The AE owns capturing it, at the stage where it's knowable. For displacements, the incumbent's renewal date is a discovery question, and the answer belongs on the opportunity next to the close date. Review your current sales process and see where it makes sense to slot in the date to your stage gates. Everything breaks when the date lives in the AE's notes and reaches services at kickoff, after the calendar is already full.

**How to tell if you 're in it:** check what share of your Q4 commit has a populated must-be-live date today.

## **Turn the commit into a capacity view**

Lay every committed and best-case deal on a calendar from Start to Must-Be-Live, apply a services effort profile by deal type, and stack the result on top of the projects already in flight. This is the same logic as [sales capacity modeling](https://revopsinflection.substack.com/p/sales-capacity-modeling-a-key-to), applied to the post-sale team. 

Run it as scenarios: commit only, commit plus best case, and everything closes on time. That's the scenario-based capacity plan the ops lead asked for, and the last scenario is the one that tells you whether a great quarter is going to hurt.

Illustrative math, not client data: if four committed deals each need roughly 300 services hours in the six weeks before the same must-be-live month, that's 1,200 hours landing on a team that may have half that free. The pileup only becomes visible once the must-be-live dates are on the calendar.

The same calendar drives the comms plan. When a new deal is going to bump an existing customer's schedule, tell that customer before the new kickoff, while you can still offer them a date you'll keep. I made a similar calendar argument from the pipeline side in [Source, Yield, Seasonality](https://revopsinflection.substack.com/p/source-yield-seasonality-build-your), and the post-sale side follows the same rule: put the dates on the calendar before you commit the resources.

**PE / Executive Reality Check:** A bookings quarter that overloads onboarding shows up later as slow time to value and early churn in the very cohort you just celebrated. That's what happened in the hypergrowth role I described above. The board sees the bookings in Q4 and the churn months later, when it's hard to trace back to the quarter that caused it.

## **Diagnostic: run these on your own data**

Before the next forecast call, run these five pulls:

1\. **Must-be-live coverage:** the share of Q4 commit and best-case deals with a must-be-live date, plus the reason behind each date.

2\. **Last year 's three dates:** close, start, and go-live for every project. Look for go-live clusters.

3\. **Effort by deal type:** services hours by deal type or size from completed projects. If you don't have this, it's your first gap.

4\. **Existing-customer load:** customer projects already scheduled in the same window as the commit's must-be-live dates.

5\. **Free capacity:** services capacity by week for the next two quarters, next to the stacked demand from pulls 1 through 4.

The deals that sign this quarter set the services calendar for next quarter, which is one more reason to [get ahead of next quarter](https://revopsinflection.substack.com/p/getting-ahead-of-next-quarter) now.

## **The move**

This week, add one field to committed and best-case deals: a must-be-live date with a reason picklist (incumbent expiry, contractual, seasonal, other). Have AEs fill it before the next forecast call, and hand the list to the services lead the same day. With eight weeks left in calendar Q4, that list gives services time to plan around the pileup before the first kickoff.

Forecast the go-live dates with the same care you give the close dates.
