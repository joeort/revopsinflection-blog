In a 2027 planning call earlier this month, a leadership team was debating whether to add account managers to work their existing customer base harder next year or allocate new reps to newer markets. I warned them about a PE-backed company I worked with that went all-in on expansion. One year they crushed their plan, and the next year they hit a total growth trough. The team was shocked, but the reality was simple: they had sold their installed base every license they possibly could (even more than they needed in some cases) without adding new products or landing new logos behind them.

When new-logo acquisition stalls, turning to your customer base looks like brilliant execution. Expansion bookings are cheaper, sales cycles are shorter, and board decks look hyper-efficient. The problem is that expansion revenue is a lagging dividend on past new-logo cohorts. If you stop feeding the top of the funnel, you are simply consuming historic yield until the base runs out of whitespace.

## The Year It Looked Great

Leaning heavily on the installed base flatters every efficiency metric a SaaS company tracks. Sales cycles shrink because you already have a MSA and an active vendor relationship. Payback periods compress, customer acquisition costs drop, and the board praises the team for capital efficient growth.

According to a [Benchmarkit 2025 report](https://www.maxio.com/blog/2025-saas-benchmark-report-trends) summarizing 2024 data, median expansion revenue rose to 40% of total new ARR, up from 25% in 2022. During that same window, the new-logo CAC ratio spiked from $1.76 in 2023 to $2.00 in 2024. As acquiring new customers grew significantly more expensive, pulling revenue out of the base looked like the only logical choice for growth teams.

## What the 40% Really Measures

A rising expansion mix is rarely a sign that your customer expansion motion suddenly became twice as effective. In most cases, it means your new-logo acquisition rate collapsed while Net Retention Rate steadily slipped. Benchmarkit reported that median NRR fell from 105% in 2021 to 101% in 2024, while companies grew at a median rate of 26% against a 35% plan [Source is same link as above].

When new ARR drops faster than expansion ARR, your mix shifts toward the base by default. Relying on that shift to hit next year's target creates an**Expansion Trough**. This is the period where your top accounts reach full penetration, but your recent new-logo cohorts are too small or too young to supply the next wave of expansion revenue. Longer term views (3-5 year plans) can include the result of product investment or M&A but those investments can take time to materialize and we're trying to hit a plan this/next year.

## The Three Gauges

To prevent your planning process from setting an invisible growth trap for 2027, you need to monitor three specific operational gauges in your GTM engine.

### Gauge 1: Mix vs. Count

This gauge measures expansion share of new ARR plotted directly alongside new-logo customer count over time. It breaks when leadership interprets an increasing expansion percentage as proof of healthy account management without checking account volume. If your expansion share rises over two consecutive years while your landed customer count declines, you are borrowing growth from future quarters.

_Note that gradually your expansion number will overtake new business._

A 105% NRR on $30M ARR with 90% GRR growing 20% a year will need $4.5M of expansion and $4.5M of new business (50/50 split) to get to the $36M. 

A 110% NRR on $50M ARR with 90% GRR growing 20% a year will need $10M of expansion and $5M of new business (33/67 split) to get to the $60M.

### Gauge 2: Whitespace

This gauge measures sold seats, sites, or modules against total addressable capacity across your top customer tiers. It breaks when planning teams assume historical expansion rates will continue automatically without auditing remaining account capacity. In a planning call this month, a team pitched raising their base expansion target based on a new offering, despite having almost no expansion history or verified whitespace data. Expansion targets must be backed by named accounts with documented, unsold capacity. For smaller orgs, find some sort of proxy for potential and refine over time.

### Gauge 3: Cohort Lag

This gauge measures the size and health of historical new-logo cohorts that are scheduled to enter their primary expansion window during the plan year. Depending on your model, expansion timelines vary: fast-follow add-ons can happen in months, strong value realization can trigger expansion by the end of year 1, but standard enterprise land-to-expand cycles average 18 to 24 months. If your 2025 and 2026 new-logo cohorts are small, your 2027 expansion target is mathematically constrained regardless of how aggressive your account managers are.

## The Other Ditch: Overshooting New Logos

Overcorrecting away from expansion creates its own operational breakdown. In a past hypergrowth role, we poured gas on new-logo acquisition so aggressively that sales outpaced customer success onboarding capacity. Customers were brought on poorly, implementations were not going well and churn spiked months later.

Sustainable growth requires balancing new-logo intake with your operational capacity to deliver initial value. Pushing too hard in either direction damages your long-term cohort mechanics. Many planning exercises focus on the new logo or AM headcount requirements but leave out all of the other key ratios (ex: SE:AE, Support:Customer/ARR, ARR/AM or CSM)

## Executive & PE Reality Check

A plan heavy on expansion flatters your operating metrics in the short term, but private equity operating partners and buyers look past the surface during commercial diligence. Buyers examine NRR degradation by cohort year alongside GRR to identify an exhausted customer base.

If diligence reveals that NRR is being carried by a shrinking group of legacy accounts while recent cohorts show low penetration and declining GRR, buyers will haircut your valuation. They recognize that they are buying a company on the edge of an expansion trough. We pulled out of a deal at a roll-up for this reason.

## Diagnostic: Three Pulls to Run This Week

Before finalizing your 2027 targets, pull these three data cuts from Salesforce to test your plan's viability:

  1. **New-Logo Count vs. Expansion Mix:** Map annual new-logo customer counts from 2022 through 2026 against expansion ARR as a percentage of total new ARR.

  2. **Top 20% Account Whitespace:** Calculate total addressable value versus current ARR across your top 20% of customers by revenue to quantify remaining headroom. Validate this with your account teams.

  3. **Cohort Lag Ratio:** Divide your 2027 expansion ARR target by the total ARR of customer cohorts currently sitting inside their primary 12-to-24-month expansion window. Is it a realistic number?




## The Move

To keep your growth engine balanced, establish a hard new-logo floor in your 2027 plan defined by account count, not just bookings.

Report and compensate teams on new-logo ARR and expansion ARR completely separately in both commission plans and board decks. Never allow expansion overperformance to paper over a shortfall in new-logo acquisition.

Expansion revenue is a dividend earned on logos you landed in previous years. Your 2027 plan must keep landing new logos today if you expect that dividend to keep paying tomorrow. If you're missing on the new business side this year, expect some growth headwinds next year unless you have other ways coming to expand into your install base (new products, new pricing, etc.)
