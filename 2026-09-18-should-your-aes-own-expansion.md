Many RevOps leaders eventually gets asked the same question by a CRO or a board member: why isn't the AE who closed this account also driving the upsell?

The answer depends on what you're actually optimizing for and too many companies never make that choice on purpose. It's a structural decision that determines whether your existing customers gets mined systematically or gets whatever attention is left over after new logo quota is chased. Get it wrong and you either starve expansion of focus or hand a low-urgency motion to a rep with no incentive to slow down and do it well.

## Three models exist

Here's when each one works and how to tell which one you're actually running versus which one you think you're running.

### Model 1: AE-led expansion. 

The AE who closed the deal stays on the account for upsell and cross-sell (usually alongside their new logo quota). I've seen it split out as distinct quotas and also as one large number and reps can get there with whatever split gets them there.

This works with a simple product, a short sales cycle, low technical complexity and a comp plan that doesn't force a tradeoff between prospecting time and account management time. It tends to hold up below $30M ARR, where the AE population is small enough that account continuity outweighs specialization.

It breaks once AE quota is weighted heavily toward new logos. Expansion becomes the thing worked between prospecting blocks and the account with strong expansion signal gets the same 20 minutes as the account with none, because the comp plan doesn't differentiate. Check your average days-to-close on expansion opportunities against new logo at the same ACV. If expansion cycles run longer, your AEs are deprioritizing them every time a new logo deal competes for attention. That's the sign you're in this model by accident.

### Model 2: CS-led expansion

Customer Success owns the renewal and the expansion conversation, on the logic that they hold the relationship and see usage signals first.

This works for high-touch, high-complexity products where adoption depth drives expansion (seat growth, module attach, usage-based upsell), and where CS is resourced as a revenue function with a real quota and comp plan, not a support function.

It breaks because most companies staff CS to retain, not to sell. A CSM with 40 accounts and a renewal-rate KPI has no structural incentive to run a competitive expansion motion, negotiate pricing or push urgency. Expansion happens as an order-taker (unless that particular CSM is commercially minded which is rare). I see this most often at $20M-$75M ARR companies that handed expansion to CS as a headcount-saving move rather than a deliberate design choice or the Board said your AEs need to focus on landing more logos. Pull expansion win rate and average discount depth by CSM. If they vary wildly by individual rather than by segment or product line, there's no CS-led expansion motion, just a few good CSMs subsidizing a model that doesn't exist.

### Model 3: dedicated account manager / expansion AE

A separate role, sometimes segmented by ACV tier, industry or product line, and owns expansion and renewal as its primary quota.

This works once the installed base supports dedicated headcount, usually north of $40M-$60M ARR though it varies by ACV, and the expansion motion is complex enough to need specialized skill: multi-stakeholder renewal negotiation, usage-based pricing conversations, competitive displacement defense.

It breaks on handoff friction. Without a structured relationship and context transfer, meaning a documented stakeholder map, deal history, and expansion signal baseline, you recreate the information loss that plagues SDR-to-AE handoffs, just later in the lifecycle. Check time-to-first-touch after a renewal handoff, and whether the AM's opening conversation references anything the AE knew. If AMs start from zero on every account, the handoff process is the problem, not the org design.

## Two variables decide the model

Ignore org chart preference and "that's how we did it at my last company." What actually matters:

  * Product complexity at the point of expansion. Is it a straightforward seat add, or does it require re-scoping value, new stakeholder buy-in, and technical validation? The closer it looks to a new sale, the more it needs sales skill and quota-driven urgency rather than relationship goodwill. I've seen thresholds where small seat adds can be done by a CSM/renewal rep but anything more complex ("complex" needs to be defined) an AE is brought in to handle the sales process.

  * Installed base size relative to new logo demand. Below a certain ARR base, dedicating headcount to expansion is a luxury you can't afford. Above it, not dedicating headcount means the highest-margin, lowest-CAC revenue in the business goes to whoever has spare capacity. 

> We managed both sides when I was a rep at SiriusDecisions. Each rep walked in the year with a $1M book and our goal was to get to 1.25M by the end of the year. If your churn ran hot, you needed to sell more new. If your install base retained well, you needed less new logos/expansion. Once 4 people had hit the $1.25M number, a new territory was created and the cycle continued. This took us from $5M-$50M before we had the tools we have access to today. Once GRR started taking a hit, we pulled additional resources to make sure clients were getting value and we weren't distracting AEs from selling.




## Wrapping it up

There's no universally correct model, only one that matches product complexity and installed base size, run by people whose comp plan rewards the behavior you want. Most companies inherit their model by accident, usually from whichever team had spare headcount when the installed base started generating real dollars. Run the diagnostic and design the motion on purpose.
