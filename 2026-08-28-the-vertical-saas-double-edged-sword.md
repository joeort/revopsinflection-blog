There are tons of software companies out there. You have your systems of record, your workflow automation, financial systems… the list goes on. Some of those are built with horizontal use cases (Notion, Clay, Salesforce, Hubspot) and others are purpose built for certain verticals (Litify, AutoCAD, Veeva). This isn't about which is right or wrong but to go deep on those vertical software companies and pros/cons of them.

#### **I. The Good: Compounding Efficiency & Defensibility**

There are reasons why so many vertical SaaS companies exist. The model works. Here are 4 areas where the good outweighs the bad:

**1\. Precise ICP Definition & Signal Quality**

From a targeting perspective, having such a tight market allows you to really niche down and understand your buyers and personas in ways horizontal plays could never do (too many variations). The data availability may be difficult but you likely know what possible sources are to obtain that information. The regulations, announcements and other signals are deeply understood by marketing and sales so knowing when and who to reach out to are higher than wider prospect bases.

The outbound messaging needs to be more ABM-like than spam cannons for these markets. You can't burn TAM when it's so limited so the messaging that goes out has to be value-add, targeted and timely. With only so many at-bats with communications in small fishing ponds, you want to be thoughtful and provide value for your audience. The events you attend/sponsor have a tighter fence around them and the words you're bidding on for paid aren't mixing in with irrelevant searches. The result should be improved messaging at a lower cost.

**2\. Purpose-Built Workflows & Measurable ROI**

Rather than providing a blank slate for users to start from, the product can pre-set workflows specific to the industry. While there will always be customization to it, having a known path to value shortens onboarding times (not always - data migrations can increase the time substantially). With CS focused on driving time to first value/magic moments, working in known sandboxes make this more targeted, repeatable and obtainable.

Those facts make the purchase decision easier to justify (not an imaginary productivity improvement, but tied to business outcomes known by the vertical). ROI calculators have much more relevant data backing them and the customer value stories are familiar to the buyers. I'm not saying selling vertical software is easy - far from it.

**The Network Effect of Industry Champion Movement**

Most of the vertical software companies I've worked with have extremely strong GRR/NRR rates. These are sticky businesses (which is why PE loves them). Users become strong, vocal champions of the tools that make them successful in their roles. The benefit here is that those champions/power users are likely to move to another company in the same vertical and can become great internal resources to get your company in the door.

Building processes and tracking of champions and users of your product can help expand your footprint. The structure needs to be there so you can proactively reach out to those contacts when they make those moves rather than wait for the Contact Us form to come in and start the cycle. Leverage existing tools (like UserGems or Champify) or build your own (via Claude/Clay) so you don't lose these valuable leads.

**Multiplying Expansion Vectors (Expanding ARPU)**

Finding your expansion path is typically easier for vertical SaaS because of the known path to value. By deeply integrating product and customers, natural upgrade paths are revealed (additional use cases, new features to move customers up the good/better/best packaging, etc). Becoming a trusted partner who knows their business built on top of a sticky product grants high NRR rates.

This tie in to existing customers keeps these companies from having to explore growth in other categories, buying groups and new markets. Having a narrow product focus allows companies to deliver even greater value, faster.

#### **II. The Curse: Friction, Concentration Risk & Market Caps**

All good things, right? To benefit from those pros, there must be cons. Let's review some of the more common scenarios that provide the vertical SaaS headwinds.

**1\. Contract Lock-In & Lengthy Replacement Cycles**

The competition is trying to benefit from the same examples above which means companies are not always in market to make changes. Vendors try to lock contracts for 3-5 years which means you may be kept out of a buying process for multiple years.

The sales process needs to start many months ahead for larger deals so cycle lengths can look extremely long as you're working with a known customer timeline. The relationship building and FUD development may begin years before you have a chance to take out a competitor. For Ops, expecting similar conversion cycles as you have in more horizontal plays don't work. The prospect base is more dependent on existing contract expiration timeframes than rep sourced pipeline creation.

**2\. High Penetration Barriers & Entrenched Incumbents**

The stickiness factor that keeps you in when you win also keeps competitors embedded. The value gains need to be enough to justify the pain to switch. Switching costs go beyond new software and implementation costs. You start hitting productivity decreases, enablement effort and systems work.

If you take a standard SaaS playbook expecting inbound to carry you, you'll be disappointed. Smaller TAMs require more robust account planning and targeted campaign activity to produce the pipeline required and stay top of mind for those companies years away from a potential change. The messaging differs for competitive take-outs vs broad pain messaging. Having the right marketing leader in place becomes a higher priority.

**3\. Market Saturation & TAM Ceilings**

Trying to model a vertical SaaS business is a different beast as well. There are a couple of big reasons that cause this:

  * 1) You'll never reach 100% penetration of a market. 

    * At automotiveMastermind, we had 2/3 of Mercedes dealerships on our platform. The other 1/3? May have been prior customers or people we've pitched recently and decided not to buy. Getting an incremental new dealer was exponentially more difficult than going into a lower penetrated OEM.

  * 2) The long tail has diminishing CAC returns

    * With a primary focus on tier 1 accounts, as companies start going down market and selling to more mid-market sized accounts, ASPs decrease and CAC is impacted. Building in a flat or decreasing spend per $ ARR will bite you in the long run.




With those 2 items in mind, this is why vertical SaaS turns more into land and expand quicker than other land-grab scenarios where logos are the ultimate measure. The natural pivot from new logo focused to expansion focus has to happen earlier as new logos slow with growth. This means having a team focused on the motion and building account plans to systematically take wallet share.

**4\. Homogeneous Value Propositions**

Messaging is a challenge today with everyone leveraging AI to drive it. Too often you can remove a company name in their messaging and replace with another and nobody would notice. The features you have, the value you drive, the terminology you're using… so are your competitors. Differentiation is a real challenge. You have to find those things that make your business unique. Be like Dwight Schrute. Dwight entered a sales call and dialed the support line of the incumbent. Dunder Mifflin's value prop was their service. Not being able to reach a human with the incumbent, he quickly dials the Dunder support line and gets a human on the line quickly. They land the sale.

Don't let the temptation to compete on price win here. Focus on the value and differentiation you can deliver without impacting margin and deal sizes. Focus on the outcomes and ROI you can provide to get away from pure cost plays.

#### **III. The PE/RevOps Playbook: Navigating the Trade-Offs**

How we attack vertical SaaS isn't like climbing Everest. With preparation, you can successfully growth the company with as little drag as possible. Focus on 3 key areas:

  1. Track contract expirations in CRM - capture this through research and call recordings. Know when customers are up for renewal and you have a compelling event. Stay in touch and provide value leading up to the decision window.

  2. Align capacity to expansion - ensure you have resources focused on expanding customers and make the comp plans clear that growing customer spend is a priority. Too often the high renewal rates lure founders in to spending less on customers since there's high likelihood to renew as-is. Build plans, hire farmers and grow through the install base faster than you would in horizontal plays.

  3. Retain pricing integrity - As you grow, add value to the product and increase prices appropriately. As existing customers come up for renewal, work to get them up to list price as quickly as possible (within contracted limits). Don't worry about losing some GRR for a high NRR return. I've seen customers get 50%+ increases. It requires strong messaging, value demonstration and delicate conversations. Just because it's hard, doesn't mean it's not worth it.




#### **IV. Go Vertical and Go Fast**

People who haven't worked for a vertical software business may be surprised that the things that work elsewhere don't work in these companies. By being aware of the benefits and headwinds, you can make adjustments to approaches and lean into the explosive potential vertical SaaS provides.
